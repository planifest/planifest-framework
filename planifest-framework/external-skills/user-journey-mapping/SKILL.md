# User Journey Mapping

You create journey maps that generate genuine insight — grounded in real user research, revealing emotional highs and lows that quantitative data obscures, and surfacing specific opportunities for design intervention.

## When to Use

- Building cross-functional empathy for the user experience before a major redesign
- Identifying the most painful moments in a multi-step user workflow
- Aligning product, design, engineering, and CS around a shared view of the user's experience

## Core Principles

**Journey maps are research artefacts, not design artefacts.** A journey map built from assumptions is a fantasy map. It should be grounded in interviews, diary studies, support data, and analytics. State your evidence sources explicitly — uncited journey maps are opinion documents.

**Emotion is the signal others miss.** Analytics tells you where users drop off; journey maps tell you how they feel about it. The emotional arc of a journey reveals moments of friction that numbers can't capture. These are design opportunities.

**Maps are tools for alignment, not just for documentation.** The most valuable outcome of a journey mapping workshop is often the shared understanding among team members who saw the same user experience differently. The artefact is secondary to the conversation it generates.

**Scope determines utility.** A journey map that covers "buying a house" is too broad to produce actionable insights. A journey map that covers "selecting a mortgage lender" is actionable. Define scope precisely — the job-to-be-done or the specific phase of a larger journey.

**Update maps when evidence changes.** A journey map from 18 months ago describes an experience that may no longer exist. Date your maps; set a review cadence.

## Approach

**Research foundation:** Conduct research before mapping. Journey maps synthesise research — they don't replace it. Relevant methods: contextual inquiry (observe users doing the task in their actual environment), diary studies (users self-report experiences over time, capturing the emotional dimension at the moment of occurrence), retrospective interviews ("walk me through the last time you had to do X"), and support ticket analysis (what frustrations do users articulate after the fact?).

**Map structure:** A journey map typically has rows for: (1) phases (the major stages of the journey), (2) steps/actions (what the user does in each phase), (3) touchpoints (channels and systems involved — web, mobile, email, phone), (4) thoughts (what the user is thinking — quotes from research), (5) emotions (the emotional state, often shown as a curve), (6) pain points (specific frustrations or friction), (7) opportunities (design interventions that could improve the experience). Not all maps need all rows — choose what's most useful for your context.

**Facilitated journey mapping workshop:** Invite stakeholders from product, design, engineering, and CS. Ground the session in research (play interview clips, read support ticket quotes). Use sticky notes to build the map collaboratively. Key exercises: (1) "What is the user doing at this step?" — separate doing from thinking and feeling; (2) "What are they thinking and feeling?" — draw on research quotes; (3) "Where are the pain points?" — prioritise by frequency and severity; (4) "What are the opportunities?" — "how might we..." reframes.

**Emotional curve:** Plot emotional valence (positive/negative) for each step of the journey. Highlight the lowest points — these are your highest-priority design opportunities. The curve is also a communication tool: showing executives a graph that drops sharply at "waiting for approval" is more compelling than a list of user complaints.

**Opportunity identification:** After mapping pain points, facilitate opportunity ideation: for each significant pain point, generate 3-5 "how might we" statements. Then prioritise opportunities by: severity of the pain, frequency of occurrence, feasibility of intervention, and strategic importance. This connects the map directly to the design backlog.

**Service blueprint extension:** For journey maps that involve organisational backstage processes (support workflows, data handoffs between systems, internal approval chains), extend the map into a service blueprint. Add rows below the user experience: frontstage actions (visible to user), backstage actions (not visible), support processes, and technology/systems. Service blueprints reveal where backstage failures cause frontstage user pain.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

- Mapping the ideal journey rather than the actual current-state journey — aspirational maps produce self-congratulation, not insight
- Using the journey map as a one-time deliverable rather than a working tool that informs ongoing design decisions
- Mapping at a resolution too high to be useful ("awareness → consideration → decision → use") without the granular step-level detail that reveals actual friction

## Output

Journey map artefact: a visual document (Figma, Miro, or printed poster) showing all rows described above, with a clear title indicating persona, scenario, and date of research. Accompanied by: an opportunity log (top 5-10 prioritised opportunities with rationale), a "moments that matter" summary (the 3 emotional peaks — highest high and lowest lows), and recommended design priorities. Shared as a living document, revisited quarterly.
