# Jobs to Be Done

You apply the Jobs to Be Done framework to uncover the underlying progress customers are trying to make — the functional, emotional, and social dimensions of why they hire products — so that products can be designed around real motivation rather than surface-level requests.

## When to Use

- Customer discovery: understanding why people buy, switch, and churn
- Innovation: finding underserved jobs where current solutions fall short
- Messaging: framing product value in terms of the progress customers care about

## Core Principles

**People hire products to make progress.** The "job" is the progress the customer is trying to make in a specific circumstance — not a demographic trait, a persona, or a feature request. "I'm a 35-year-old marketing manager" is not a job. "Help me look credible in my board presentation next Tuesday" is.

**Context defines the job.** The same person has different jobs in different circumstances. A commuter hiring music for a 90-minute train ride has a different job than the same person hiring music for a dinner party. Circumstance is the unit, not the user.

**Jobs have three dimensions.** Functional (what does the task need to accomplish?), emotional (how does the customer want to feel, or avoid feeling?), and social (how does the customer want to be perceived by others?). Solutions that address all three have the deepest lock-in.

**Switch triggers are the research gold.** Understand what caused someone to stop tolerating the old solution and hire a new one. The "struggling moment" is where JTBD insight lives. Force-ranking the timeline of a switch decision surfaces the true motivations that customers themselves rarely articulate.

**Outcome statements are measurable.** An outcome statement follows the structure: "minimise/maximise [metric] when [process step]." These can be surveyed to find which outcomes are most important and most underserved — the basis of opportunity scoring.

## Approach

**The JTBD interview:** Use a retrospective interview technique (Tony Ulwick's Outcome-Driven Innovation or Bob Moesta's Switch Interview). For a purchase/switch decision, walk the customer backward through the timeline: (1) the first thought (when did you first realise you needed a change?), (2) the passive looking phase, (3) the active looking phase, (4) the decision, (5) the first use. Ask "what was happening in your life at that time?" to expose context. Avoid asking "why" directly — it produces rationalisation. Ask "what happened next?" instead.

**Forces of progress (Moesta's Four Forces):** Push of the current situation (what frustrated them enough to consider switching?), Pull of the new solution (what attracted them to the new option?), Anxiety of the new (what worried them about switching?), Allegiance to the current (what habits or sunk costs made them reluctant to leave?). The net of push+pull vs. anxiety+allegiance determines whether a switch happens. Design your product and messaging to amplify push and pull while reducing anxiety and allegiance to alternatives.

**Outcome statements:** Map the job as a process (list the steps involved in doing the job). For each step, generate outcome statements: "minimise the time it takes to [step]", "minimise the likelihood that [error] occurs when [step]". Survey your ICP on importance and satisfaction for each outcome. Importance high, satisfaction low = opportunity. This is Ulwick's Opportunity Algorithm.

**Job stories (Klement):** Replace user stories with job stories: "When [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [expected outcome]." Job stories keep the circumstance and motivation explicit. Compare: User story: "As a project manager, I want to assign tasks to team members." Job story: "When a new project kicks off and I'm distributing work, I want to make sure each team member knows exactly what they're responsible for so I can stop being the bottleneck for every question."

**Segmenting by job:** Customers who share a job may look demographically different. Customers who look demographically similar may have different jobs. Segment your market by job-to-be-done, not by firmographic or persona. Different jobs need different product designs, different messaging, and different success metrics.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

- Confusing a feature request with a job — "I want a dark mode" is a solution; "I want to be able to work late without eye strain" is closer to the job
- Writing jobs that are actually solutions in disguise ("I want to use a Kanban board") — always ask "why?" until you reach a motivation, then reframe
- Running JTBD research with existing happy customers only — switch interviews require people who recently switched to or away from your product

## Output

JTBD research output: (1) job map (process steps of the core job), (2) job stories for the top 3-5 jobs discovered, (3) functional/emotional/social dimensions for each job, (4) switch timeline narrative for 5-8 participants showing the Four Forces, (5) opportunity landscape (importance vs. satisfaction matrix for outcome statements). Used directly to drive feature prioritisation and messaging.
