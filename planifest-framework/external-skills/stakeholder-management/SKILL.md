# Stakeholder Management

You manage stakeholders as a discipline of relationship-building, clear communication, and structured influence — not as political manoeuvring, but as the work of creating the conditions for good decisions.

## When to Use

- Cross-functional initiatives with competing priorities and no single owner
- Situations where you need commitment without formal authority over the people you need
- Escalation decisions: when to raise issues and how to frame them

## Core Principles

**Stakeholder maps before stakeholder meetings.** Identify everyone with a stake, their success metrics, their concerns, and their influence level before you start the engagement process. Blind-siding a powerful stakeholder mid-project is a strategy-killer.

**Alignment is built, not announced.** Don't call a meeting to "present the decision." Do the pre-work: share context, understand objections, and incorporate legitimate concerns before the meeting. Meetings confirm alignment; they don't build it.

**Influence comes from credibility.** Credibility comes from following through on commitments, communicating proactively about problems, and demonstrating you understand others' constraints. Manage this as a balance that compounds over time.

**Conflict surfaces better decisions.** Disagreement between stakeholders is not a problem to suppress — it's information about competing constraints. Surface it early; don't smooth it over until it explodes later.

**Escalation is a tool, not a failure.** Knowing when to escalate and how to do it cleanly is a skill. Escalating too late is worse than escalating too early.

## Approach

**Stakeholder mapping (RACI + power/interest grid):** Use RACI (Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, Informed) for role clarity, and a power/interest grid (high/low power × high/low interest) to calibrate engagement depth. High-power/high-interest stakeholders need regular direct engagement. High-power/low-interest stakeholders need clear, concise updates — don't waste their time. Low-power/high-interest stakeholders are your advocates — invest in them.

**Pre-wiring decisions:** Before any significant decision meeting, brief the key stakeholders individually. Share the draft decision, explain the reasoning, and ask: "What's your biggest concern?" Incorporate legitimate concerns; document and address concerns you can't incorporate. By the time the decision meeting happens, you should know who will object and have responses prepared.

**Building the narrative:** Stakeholders remember stories better than logic. Frame your ask in their terms: "This investment will reduce your team's support escalations by 30%" lands better than "this will improve the UX." Map each stakeholder's success metric and translate your proposal into their language.

**Handling resistance:** Distinguish between resistance types: (1) data disagreement — share evidence, invite counterevidence; (2) values disagreement — acknowledge the difference, find shared goals; (3) political resistance — understand the underlying interest, find a face-saving path. Don't argue with someone who is actually protecting their team's budget — find out what they need and whether you can give it.

**Escalation:** Escalate when: you've made a good-faith attempt to resolve at your level, the decision is time-sensitive, or the decision is material enough that the person above you would want to know. When escalating, bring the options and your recommendation — not just the problem. "I need you to decide between X and Y because we've been unable to align at our level; I recommend X for these reasons" is a clean escalation.

**Communication cadence:** Err toward over-communication on status, especially when things are going wrong. A proactive "we've hit a blocker and here's how we're handling it" message builds more trust than a reactive explanation after a missed deadline.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

- Treating stakeholder management as a one-time activity rather than an ongoing practice that requires consistent investment
- Presenting recommendations to stakeholders who are hearing the context for the first time in the meeting
- Conflating influence with persuasion — influence is about helping people make better decisions, not getting them to say yes to yours

## Output

Stakeholder management outputs are mostly process, not documents. Concrete deliverables when needed: a stakeholder map (who, role, interest, concerns, engagement plan), a pre-read document for significant decisions, and a RACI for complex projects. Communication norms (cadence, format) should be agreed at project start and referenced throughout.
