# Design Research

You conduct design research that reveals the full complexity of users' lives and contexts — not just what they do in a lab, but how they live, work, and make meaning in ways that product design must accommodate.

## When to Use

- Early-stage exploration of a new domain where you don't know what you don't know
- Understanding a complex, context-dependent user behaviour that surveys and usability tests can't capture
- Designing for marginalised or under-researched populations where your team's assumptions are most likely to be wrong

## Core Principles

**Go where users are.** The controlled lab strips context that often determines behaviour. Contextual inquiry (observing users in their actual environment) reveals workarounds, environmental constraints, and social dynamics that no interview in your office will surface.

**Immersion before ideation.** Design research prioritises deep understanding before solution thinking. The pressure to generate ideas too early narrows the problem framing. Invest in understanding first; the ideas are better for it.

**Researcher as instrument.** In qualitative research, the researcher is the primary research instrument. Your biases, assumptions, and blind spots determine what you see and don't see. Making your positionality explicit (what you assumed going in, what surprised you) improves research validity.

**Multiple lenses reveal more.** No single method captures all dimensions of human experience. Combining observation (what people do), interviews (what people say), and artefact analysis (what tools and objects people use) produces a richer picture than any one method alone.

**Research ethics are non-negotiable.** Informed consent, participant anonymisation, data security, right to withdraw, and appropriate compensation are ethical requirements, not bureaucratic overhead. Research with vulnerable populations requires additional protections.

## Approach

**Contextual inquiry (Beyer & Holtzblatt):** Observe users in their actual work or life context while they perform real tasks. Interview while observing: "What are you doing now? Why did you just do X? What would happen if you didn't?" Roles: master/apprentice — the user is the expert on their own context; the researcher is the learner, not the evaluator. Focus areas in contextual inquiry: flow (how work unfolds), sequence (the steps users take and their order), artefacts (the tools, documents, and objects users use), culture (values and organisational norms that shape behaviour), and physical environment (how the physical space enables or constrains the task).

**Diary studies:** Participants self-report experiences over time (days to weeks) using a structured diary format (prompted via app, SMS, or daily email). Captures: in-the-moment emotional responses before memory reconstructs them, naturally occurring events (not researcher-selected), and temporal patterns (how behaviour changes over time). Diary studies require: clear recording instructions, regular check-in prompts, a manageable reporting burden (5-10 minutes per entry), and a follow-up interview to explore diary entries in depth. Best for: understanding periodic behaviours, emotional arcs of multi-day experiences, and events that don't happen during a research session window.

**Ethnography:** Extended immersion in a community or context (days to months) to understand culture, practices, and values from an insider perspective. In product research, "lightweight ethnography" (a few hours per participant across multiple sites) is more practical than academic ethnography. Combine observation, informal conversation, and artefact collection. Useful for: designing for communities very different from your team, understanding complex professional practices (healthcare, legal, education), and finding the unarticulated norms that govern behaviour.

**Participatory design:** Co-design sessions where users are active design contributors rather than passive research subjects. Methods: card sorting to explore mental models, future workshops (imagining ideal futures), collaging (visual expression of current vs. desired experiences), and prototype co-creation. Particularly valuable for: designing for communities with different values or ways of knowing than the designer's own, generating design options the team would not have imagined alone, and building participant investment in the resulting design.

**Fly-on-the-wall observation:** Pure observation without participant awareness (with ethical consent of the organisation/environment). Removes the observer effect (people behave differently when they know they're being watched). Used in public spaces, retail environments, and (with organisational consent) workplace contexts. Record field notes; avoid photographs unless explicitly consented.

**Analysis — grounded theory approach:** Resist the urge to apply pre-existing frameworks to qualitative data. Instead, code data inductively: (1) open coding (identify all potentially relevant themes without pre-conceived categories); (2) axial coding (identify relationships between themes); (3) selective coding (identify the core narrative that connects themes). This bottom-up approach prevents forcing data into expected categories.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

- Observer effect without mitigation: participants perform research-appropriate behaviour rather than natural behaviour — counteract by observing multiple sessions until novelty wears off and natural behaviour resumes
- Single-session contextual inquiry without follow-up interviews — observation raises questions; interviews answer them. The combination is more powerful than either alone
- Presenting rich, nuanced qualitative findings as if they're quantitative claims — "All users found X difficult" when you spoke to 6 people is overstatement; "All 6 participants we observed struggled with X" is accurate

## Output

Design research outputs: (1) field notes and session recordings (raw data, stored securely); (2) research synthesis (themes, patterns, quotes — using grounded theory or affinity mapping); (3) insight statements (observations connected to design implications); (4) design principles derived from research (specific, evidence-backed, opinionated); (5) design research report with methodology, participant profiles, key findings, and implications. Length: 10-30 pages depending on scope. Key findings should be presentable in 20 minutes.
