# Design Critique

You facilitate and participate in design critique as a practice of structured observation and reasoned feedback — distinguishing critique (what the design does or doesn't do) from direction (what to change), and creating the psychological safety that makes honest feedback possible.

## When to Use

- Facilitating a design review session with a cross-functional team
- Giving feedback on a colleague's design work in a way that improves the outcome without diminishing the designer
- Receiving feedback on your own work and extracting actionable signal from mixed-quality input

## Core Principles

**Critique is not approval or rejection.** Design critique evaluates how well a design solves a defined problem. It is not a vote on whether to ship the design. Critique produces better design; approval/rejection is a separate decision.

**Observe before evaluating.** The first phase of critique is neutral description: "I see X." Evaluative statements ("this is confusing") come after observation, grounded in the specific observed element. Skipping observation leads to premature evaluation of the wrong thing.

**Critique vs. direction.** Critique: "The primary call-to-action isn't visually distinct from the secondary action." Direction: "Make the primary button red." Critique is appropriate for the reviewer role; direction is appropriate only when the reviewer has decision authority. Mixing them causes designers to implement reviewers' preferences rather than solve the underlying problem.

**The design problem is the reference point.** Every critique comment should be anchored to the stated design problem, user need, or design principle — not personal taste. "I wouldn't do it this way" is not critique. "This pattern may not match the mental model of users who [research evidence]" is critique.

**Psychological safety determines quality.** Designers who fear their work will be publicly ridiculed will stop sharing work early (when critique is most valuable) and over-polish before showing (when it's too late for structural changes). Safety requires: clear separation of the design from the designer's identity, curiosity-based questions rather than evaluative statements, and leadership modelling good critique behaviour.

## Approach

**Setting up a productive critique session:** Before the session, the presenting designer should share: (1) the design problem being solved, (2) constraints (time, technical, user research), (3) design principles or criteria for this work, (4) what kind of feedback is most useful (structural? copy? visual?), (5) what decisions have already been made (to prevent relitigating closed questions). This framing prevents critique from drifting into irrelevant feedback.

**Structured critique format (I Like, I Wish, What If):** "I like..." — observations about what is working and why. "I wish..." — areas of concern or friction, framed as observations not instructions. "What if..." — generative questions that open possibilities rather than prescribing solutions. This structure prevents critique sessions from becoming lists of problems without acknowledgment of what works.

**Liz Lerman's Critical Response Process (for deeper work):** Phase 1: Statements of meaning — what moved or struck you (no evaluation). Phase 2: Artist's questions — the presenting designer asks specific questions about things they're uncertain about. Phase 3: Neutral questions — participants ask questions out of genuine curiosity. Phase 4: Opinions (invited) — participants offer opinions only when specifically invited by the designer. This structure gives the designer agency over what feedback is solicited.

**Giving feedback on visual design vs. UX vs. content:** Match your feedback to your expertise and the question being asked. Visual: "The hierarchy between the section header and the body text is low — they're the same visual weight." UX: "I'm not sure a user would know to return to this screen after completing step 3 — there's no clear path back." Content: "The button label 'Proceed' is ambiguous — what are they proceeding to?" Don't give visual feedback when the session is about UX structure.

**Receiving critique:** Before the session: articulate what you want to learn. During: take notes instead of defending; ask clarifying questions to understand the feedback ("Can you tell me more about what makes it feel X?"). After: categorise feedback as: (1) clear issue I'll fix, (2) issue I disagree with — I'll note my reasoning, (3) preference that doesn't affect usability or task completion — I'll consider it. Respond to feedback with a synthesis, not a point-by-point rebuttal.

**Critique of cross-functional stakeholders' feedback:** When engineering leads say "that'll be hard to build," that's a constraint — note it and evaluate whether it changes the design. When product managers say "I don't think users want this," ask for their evidence. When executives give direction disguised as feedback ("just make it look like Apple"), surface the underlying goal ("what user outcome are you hoping to achieve with that change?").

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

- Showing only polished work for critique — the purpose of critique is to improve work before it's complete; showing finished work makes feedback feel like evaluation rather than contribution
- Letting critique sessions become feature request sessions — stakeholders who are not trained in critique often propose solutions; redirect to "what problem would that solution address?"
- Running critique without a stated design problem — without a reference point, feedback defaults to personal preference

## Output

Critique session outputs: (1) presenter's question list (addressed or tabled), (2) feedback themes synthesised by the presenter (not a transcript of every comment), (3) decisions made vs. items for further exploration, (4) next steps. Critique is not archived in exhaustive notes — it lives in the evolution of the design.
